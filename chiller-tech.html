Distinct differences captured from the comparison table

Compressor control
Old: fixed-speed / staged
Reciprocating and older centrifugal systems typically rely on on-off cycling, unloading steps, or limited staging, while newer machines use VSD logic and tighter digital modulation.
Efficiency focus
Old: full-load bias
Newer chillers are optimized for part-load operation, which matters more in real building plants because chillers rarely stay at nameplate load all day.
Maintenance profile
Old: oil and wear heavier
Legacy systems usually bring more vibration, more oil management, and more manual service, while oil-free designs reduce friction losses and maintenance points.
System intelligence
Old: basic controls
Modern chillers integrate sensors, alarms, trending, BAS/BMS integration, and analytics so operators can optimize sequencing and diagnose faults faster.

Old chiller types

What “old” means here

These are the mature or legacy-leaning technologies that defined earlier mechanical plants: reciprocating, standard centrifugal, standard screw, and absorption as the alternate heat-driven branch.

TypeCore technologyStrengthsWeaknesses
ReciprocatingPiston compressor, staged or on/offSimple concept, lower upfront cost, flexible in smaller systemsNoisy, high vibration, more maintenance, older efficiency profile
CentrifugalDynamic impeller compressionLarge capacity, strong full-load efficiency, central-plant friendlyLow-load performance can be weaker without modern controls
ScrewTwin helical rotorsReliable, fewer moving parts, good part-load behaviorHigher first cost than reciprocating, mature rather than cutting-edge
AbsorptionHeat-driven refrigeration cycleUseful with steam or waste heat, lowers electric peak demandLower COP, larger footprint, more complex thermal integration
Reciprocating
Best understood as the piston-era baseline
Strong for simple plants, but it pays a penalty in acoustics, wear, and lifecycle service.
Centrifugal
The classic large-plant machine
Still relevant, but older units are less agile than modern VSD or oil-free variants.
Screw
The workhorse mature compressor
Common in mid-size buildings because it balances size, reliability, and control reasonably well.
Absorption
A different path, not just an old one
Important when thermal energy is available, but it is not the default choice for standard electric plants.
Distinct takeaway
Older chiller technology usually means more mechanical simplicity and sometimes lower capital cost, but also more maintenance exposure and weaker optimization under variable building load.

New chiller types

What “new” means here

Newer chillers do not just mean newer manufacture dates. They mean newer control philosophy, stronger part-load optimization, better motor speed control, and in premium models, oil-free magnetic bearing architecture.

TypeCore technologyStrengthsWeaknesses
ScrollSpiral compression, often modularizedCompact, quiet, efficient for small-to-medium loadsNot the best fit for very large plants
Oil-free centrifugalMagnetic bearings, no oil loopLow friction, low maintenance, no oil fouling, high efficiencyHigher capital cost and more specialized service
Turbocor / VSD centrifugalVariable-speed high-speed compressionExcellent part-load performance, fast modulation, smart sequencingHigher electronics complexity and premium pricing
Modular chillerMultiple smaller units acting as one plantScalable, redundant, easy phased expansionMore modules can mean more components to coordinate
Scroll
The compact mainstream option
Often favored for rooftops, packaged plants, and modular arrays where simplicity and footprint matter.
Oil-free centrifugal
Premium low-friction architecture
Removes oil-management penalties and usually improves service intervals and heat-exchanger cleanliness.
Turbocor / VSD
Modern response to variable-load reality
The strongest improvement is often real operating efficiency, not only headline full-load numbers.
Modular
System architecture as an advantage
Useful where redundancy, retrofit space, or staged expansion matters more than one big machine.
Distinct takeaway
Newer chiller technology is defined by control quality, part-load efficiency, reduced oil dependence, and deeper BMS/BAS visibility rather than by compressor shape alone.

Heat rejection: air-cooled versus water-cooled

Air-cooled
Simpler plant, lower auxiliary complexity
  • Rejects heat directly to ambient air using condenser fans.
  • Best where water treatment, towers, and condenser loops are undesirable.
  • Usually easier to install and maintain, but less efficient in hot ambient conditions.
Water-cooled
Higher-efficiency central plant path
  • Rejects heat through condenser water and a cooling tower.
  • Better suited to large loads, high operating hours, and efficiency-driven plants.
  • More components, more treatment, and more integration work, but stronger performance at scale.
Distinct difference
This is not an old-versus-new distinction by itself. It is a system architecture choice. Older or newer compressor technologies can exist in either air-cooled or water-cooled arrangements, but water-cooled plants usually dominate where capacity and operating efficiency are priorities.

Technology shift from old to new

Control logic
Fixed speed → variable speed
Smoother loading, less cycling stress, and better plant matching.
Maintenance
Oil-heavy → oil-free options
Less fouling and fewer lubrication-related service issues in premium designs.
Optimization
Full-load → part-load focus
Modern plants are judged by real operating profile, not only design-point performance.
Controls ecosystem
Standalone → connected intelligence
Trending, alarms, remote visibility, and BAS coordination become standard expectations.
What this means in practice
When you evaluate a replacement or retrofit, the real gain usually comes from better sequencing, better part-load response, softer starting, lower maintenance exposure, and better operational data rather than from changing the compressor family alone.
